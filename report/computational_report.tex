\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This is a companion technical report to the manuscript \emph{``On
parametric Maximum Closure Problems over precedence forests''} (Dose,
Furini and Locatelli). The manuscript introduces the algorithms studied
here (\texttt{PaC}, \texttt{DPaC}, \texttt{HPaC}, \texttt{DHPaC},
\texttt{HIPaC}, \texttt{HOPaC} and \texttt{RaC}) together with their
correctness proofs and complexity analysis; this report contains the full
computational study -- instance design, validation methodology, benchmark
campaigns and results -- at a level of detail appropriate for a technical
report rather than for the manuscript itself. \Cref{sec:recap} recaps the
problem and the algorithms compactly, for a reader without the manuscript
at hand; \Cref{sec:computational_results} is the complete computational
study.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem and algorithms recap}\label{sec:recap}

\subsection{The parametric Maximum Closure Problem}

Let $G=(V,A)$ be a directed acyclic graph on $n$ items $V=\{1,\dots,n\}$,
where arc $(i,j)\in A$ means that including item $i$ forces the inclusion
of item $j$. Each item $i$ has an integer profit $p_i$ (possibly negative)
and a strictly positive integer weight $w_i$. For a closed subset $S$
(i.e.\ $i\in S\Rightarrow j\in S$ for every $(i,j)\in A$), let
$P(S)=\sum_{i\in S}p_i$ and $W(S)=\sum_{i\in S}w_i$. The parametric Maximum
Closure Problem asks for the set of optimal solutions of
$u(\lambda):=\max_{S\text{ closed}}P(S)-\lambda W(S)$ as a function of
$\lambda\in\mathbb R$. Function $u$ is convex and piecewise linear, with at
most $n$ breakpoints $\lambda_1>\dots>\lambda_k$; the solutions at
consecutive breakpoints are nested, inducing a partition of $V$ into $k$
disjoint \emph{closure layers} $\mathcal I_1,\dots,\mathcal I_k$, strictly
decreasing in profit-to-weight ratio $P_r/W_r$. Computing this ordered
partition -- the \emph{optimal sequence of closure layers} -- is the
computational task studied here. The manuscript restricts $G$ to be a
\emph{directed forest} (the underlying undirected graph is a forest, arc
orientation otherwise arbitrary) and gives algorithms specific to that
case; this report benchmarks those algorithms empirically.

\subsection{Algorithms benchmarked}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2cm,style=nextline]
  \item[\texttt{PaC}] Peel-and-Contract: the manuscript's direct-scan
  reference algorithm for arbitrarily oriented forests, $O(n^2)$ worst-case
  time, $O(n)$ space; finds closure layers in decreasing ratio order by
  repeatedly peeling the current best final nodes or contracting an arc.
  \item[\texttt{DPaC}] the dual variant of \texttt{PaC}, finding closure layers
  in increasing ratio order; same asymptotic complexity and scan-based,
  non-heap structure.
  \item[\texttt{HPaC}] the heap-based practical variant of \texttt{PaC}:
  same worst-case complexity in general ($O(n^2\log n)$), but replaces
  linear scans with a lazy heap, which the manuscript shows is faster in
  practice on most (not all) topologies.
  \item[\texttt{DHPaC}] the dual of \texttt{HPaC}.
  \item[\texttt{HIPaC} / \texttt{HOPaC}] specialized heap-based variants for
  forests known in advance to be entirely in-trees (out-degree $\le1$
  everywhere) or out-trees (in-degree $\le1$ everywhere) respectively;
  $O(n\log n)$ worst-case time in both cases.
  \item[\texttt{RaC}] Rake-and-Compress: a top-tree-based algorithm with
  $O(n\log n)$ time and space, derived in the manuscript following
  suggestions from \texttt{Claude Fable 5}; applicable to general trees
  regardless of orientation.
  \item[\texttt{BPPF}] Bounded-Precision Parametric Pseudoflow, the
  unmodified upstream implementation of Hochbaum's parametric pseudoflow
  method for general precedence graphs (not restricted to forests); used
  here purely as a third-party, independent correctness oracle, and at a
  scope-limited size as a general parametric-flow reference point.
\end{description}

Every algorithm returns the same canonical object -- the ordered sequence
of closure layers with their exact rational thresholds -- computed with exact
integer/rational arithmetic throughout (no floating point in any decision
path). Full derivations, pseudocode and complexity proofs for
\texttt{PaC}/\texttt{DPaC}/\texttt{HPaC}/\texttt{DHPaC}/\texttt{HIPaC}/\texttt{HOPaC}
and \texttt{RaC} are in the manuscript; this report takes them as given
and evaluates them empirically.

\section{Computational Results}\label{sec:computational_results}

\subsection{Instance generation}\label{subsec:v2_instances}

Every instance is stored in a small, capacity-free text format: a forest on
$n$ items given by its arc list, one integer profit $p_i$ (possibly
negative) and one strictly positive integer weight $w_i$ per item $i$, where
arc $(u,v)$ encodes the closure implication $x_u\le x_v$. No capacity field
exists anywhere in this format and no reader is allowed to accept one. Every
instance file is produced by a seeded, deterministic Python generator --
never hand-edited -- and is fully determined by four independent choices: a
\emph{topology} (\cref{tab:v2_topologies}), an \emph{affine-coefficient
family} (\cref{tab:v2_coeff_families}), a size $n$, and a random seed.

The first four families give, respectively: dispersed positive ratios
(\texttt{independent-positive}); the same but with some items locally
unfavorable, since $p_i$ may be negative (\texttt{independent-signed});
ratios concentrated near $1$ (\texttt{correlated}); and more heterogeneous
ratios obtained by anti-correlating $p_i$ with $w_i$
(\texttt{anti-correlated}). The last two stress exact rational comparisons
directly: for \texttt{near-ties}, a target ratio $\mathrm{num}/\mathrm{den}$
is drawn once per instance ($\mathrm{num},\mathrm{den}$ uniform on
$\{1,\dots,20\}$); for every item $i$, $t_i$ is uniform on $\{1,\dots,50\}$,
$w_i=\mathrm{den}\cdot t_i$ and $p_i=\mathrm{num}\cdot t_i+\jmath_i$ with
jitter $\jmath_i$ uniform on $\{-2,-1,1,2\}$, so many items share a common
ratio up to a small integer perturbation. For \texttt{exact-ties}, the $n$
items are split into $\max(1,\lfloor n/20\rfloor)$ groups, each assigned its
own exact ratio $\mathrm{num}_g/\mathrm{den}_g$ (drawn uniformly on
$\{1,\dots,20\}^2$); for an item $i$ in group $g$, $t_i$ is uniform on
$\{1,\dots,50\}$, $w_i=\mathrm{den}_g\cdot t_i$ and $p_i=\mathrm{num}_g\cdot
t_i$, so every item in a group shares the exact same ratio
$p_i/w_i=\mathrm{num}_g/\mathrm{den}_g$ -- stressing the canonicalization
rule that merges consecutive closure layers at an equal threshold.

\subsection{Experimental goals and research questions}\label{subsec:v2_goals}

The computational study answers six questions about algorithms for the
parametric maximum-closure problem on directed forests, each with its own
subsection below:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Is the heap-based scan (\texttt{HPaC}) actually preferable to the
  direct-scan reference algorithm (\texttt{PaC}), and are there structured
  cases where the opposite holds? (\cref{subsec:v2_fma_vs_hfma})
  \item How does \texttt{HPaC} scale empirically on directed forests with
  arbitrary orientation? (\cref{subsec:v2_hfma_scaling})
  \item How much do the specialized heap variants for in-forests
  (\texttt{HIPaC}) and out-forests (\texttt{HOPaC}) gain over the general
  \texttt{HPaC}/\texttt{DHPaC} pair when the orientation is known in advance?
  (\cref{subsec:v2_specialized})
  \item How do \texttt{HPaC} and the rake-and-compress algorithm
  (\texttt{RaC}) compare on random forests?
  (\cref{subsec:v2_hfma_vs_rac})
  \item Which structural families favor \texttt{RaC} and which favor
  \texttt{HPaC}? (\cref{subsec:v2_structured})
  \item How do these forest-specific algorithms compare with a general
  parametric-flow method not restricted to forests?
  (\cref{subsec:v2_bppf})
\end{enumerate}

Every subsection below is backed by a complete campaign with a preregistered
instance matrix, or is explicitly marked exploratory where it is not.

\subsection{Algorithms and implementations}\label{subsec:v2_algorithms}

All algorithms compared here are implemented natively for the closure model
of the accompanying manuscript's forest-closure model (integral profit $p_i$, strictly
positive integral weight $w_i$, affine node cost $p_i-\lambda w_i$, arc
$(u,v)$ meaning $x_u\le x_v$): there is no capacity, no relaxed linear
program and no reconstruction of a knapsack solution anywhere in the
implementation.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{PaC}/\texttt{DPaC}: the direct-scan reference implementation
  of the manuscript's main forest algorithm and its dual, for arbitrarily oriented forests.
  \texttt{DPaC} shares \texttt{PaC}'s scan-based, non-heap structure and its
  empirical $O(n^2)$-like scaling (\cref{subsec:v2_hfma_scaling}).
  \item \texttt{HPaC}/\texttt{DHPaC}: the heap-based practical variant and
  its dual, for arbitrarily oriented forests. Both are exercised throughout
  \cref{subsec:v2_fma_vs_hfma,subsec:v2_hfma_scaling,subsec:v2_hfma_vs_rac,subsec:v2_structured}.
  \texttt{DHPaC} is consistently slower than \texttt{HPaC} on mixed-orientation
  random forests -- median \texttt{DHPaC}/\texttt{HPaC} is
  $\approx 1.4$--$1.55$ on the medium campaign (B) and $\approx 1.6$--$1.7$
  on the large campaign (C), stable across sizes -- but the two remain within
  the same order of magnitude and neither changes which algorithm family
  (heap-based general vs.\ \texttt{RaC}) is preferable; \texttt{DHPaC} is
  reported alongside \texttt{HPaC} rather than in a separate subsection for
  this reason, not because the two are numerically close.
  \item \texttt{HIPaC}/\texttt{HOPaC}: the specialized heap-based variants for
  in-forests and out-forests, respectively.
  \item \texttt{RaC}: the rake-and-compress/top-tree implementation of the
  algorithm described in the accompanying manuscript, ported from an
  experimental package and audited operation-by-operation
  against that source and against the manuscript
  (\texttt{docs/RAC\_AUDIT.md}).
  \item \texttt{BPPF}: the unmodified upstream bounded-precision parametric
  pseudoflow implementation, used both as an independent verification oracle
  (\cref{subsec:v2_validation}) and, at a scope-limited size, as a general
  parametric-flow baseline (\cref{subsec:v2_bppf}).
\end{itemize}

Every algorithm returns the same canonical object: the ordered sequence of
closure layers, their exact rational thresholds, and the induced closure at
every breakpoint. All rational comparisons use exact signed 64-bit numerators
and denominators compared via signed 128-bit cross-multiplication; no
floating-point value appears anywhere in an algorithm's decision path.

\subsection{Validation methodology}\label{subsec:v2_validation}

Correctness is established independently of any single algorithm and before
any timing claim:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Exhaustive enumeration.} For every directed forest with at
  most four items over a finite affine-coefficient grid, and for thousands
  of random forests up to eleven items, every algorithm's output is checked
  against an independent oracle that enumerates all closed sets, builds
  their affine lines and computes the exact upper envelope.
  \item \textbf{Maximum closure at a fixed $\lambda$, via an independent
  max-flow engine.} For a chosen rational $\lambda$, the instance is
  reduced to the standard maximum-weight-closure network and solved with
  \texttt{BPPF}, a third-party pseudoflow implementation unrelated to any
  algorithm above; agreement with our own algorithms' closure at that
  $\lambda$ was checked at every breakpoint midpoint across random, path and
  star topologies and all six affine-coefficient families, with zero
  disagreements before \texttt{BPPF} was trusted for
  \cref{subsec:v2_validation,subsec:v2_bppf}.
  \item \textbf{Cross-algorithm differential agreement.} Every official
  campaign benchmarks multiple algorithms on the same instance and compares
  their returned sequences (partition, thresholds and canonical order, not
  only the closure layer count); any disagreement is reported explicitly rather
  than averaged away.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental environment and protocol}\label{subsec:v2_protocol}

All algorithms are implemented in C++ and run single-threaded. Experiments
were run on a Linux machine (Ubuntu 22.04.5, kernel 6.8) equipped with an
Intel Core i7-12700 processor and 32\,GB of RAM, GCC 11.4.0 at \texttt{-O3}.
Every benchmark process is pinned to one physical core; algorithm order is
independently shuffled per repetition so no algorithm is systematically
favored by CPU frequency state. Only the algorithm call itself is timed:
instance parsing, correctness verification and result serialization are
excluded from every reported time. Headline comparisons are always paired
per instance (the same instance, same process-invocation window, for every
algorithm compared), and reported as the median and interquartile range of
the per-instance ratio, not a ratio of aggregate means. A preregistered
wall-clock timeout (300\,s) and a memory ceiling (8\,GB, enforced with
\texttt{ulimit -v}) apply to every run; an instance that hits either is
recorded as a censored observation, never silently dropped or estimated.
The machine's CPU governor could not be set to \texttt{performance} (no
passwordless root); this is a documented source of noise in absolute
wall-clock numbers, mitigated by reporting paired ratios rather than raw
times (\texttt{docs/EXPERIMENTAL\_PROTOCOL.md}, "Known measurement
limitation").

All numbers below are computed by \texttt{tools/build\_report.sh} directly
from \texttt{results/raw/*.csv} (raw) and \texttt{results/processed/} (paired
medians and ratios); none is hand-typed. Every campaign in
\cref{subsec:v2_instances} was run to completion (or to its preregistered
censoring point) exactly as specified in \texttt{docs/EXPERIMENTAL\_PROTOCOL.md}.
Across all 9\,000 benchmarked instances and 203\,880 timed runs spanning
campaigns B, C, D and E, \textbf{every algorithm pair benchmarked on the same
instance returned the identical closure layer sequence}: zero disagreements
(\cref{tab:v2_correctness}).

\subsection{PaC versus HPaC}\label{subsec:v2_fma_vs_hfma}

On mixed-orientation random forests (campaign B, $n\in\{100,\dots,1000\}$,
2\,400 instances), \texttt{HPaC} and \texttt{PaC} are comparable at small
$n$ and \texttt{HPaC} pulls ahead only as $n$ grows, consistently with the
manuscript's theoretical prediction that a heap cannot improve
\texttt{HPaC}'s worst-case complexity but should help in practice: median
\texttt{PaC}/\texttt{HPaC} is $0.78$ at $n=100$ (\texttt{PaC} is in fact
marginally faster here, where heap-maintenance overhead outweighs its
benefit), crosses $1$ around $n\approx 400$, and reaches $1.72\times$ at
$n=1\,000$ (\texttt{campaign\_b\_pac\_over\_hpac.tex}), consistent with
\texttt{PaC}'s scan-based construction paying a growing cost per breakpoint
as $n$ increases while \texttt{HPaC}'s heap does not.
\texttt{DPaC} tracks \texttt{PaC} closely, as expected from sharing its scan
structure, and opens no separate storyline.

The countercase anticipated by the manuscript is confirmed quantitatively on
mixed-orientation stars (campaign D, PaC-eligible subset,
$n\in\{100,\dots,2000\}$): \texttt{HPaC} is \emph{dramatically slower} than
\texttt{PaC} at every size tested (\cref{tab:v2_star_fma}), by a median
factor growing from $17.2\times$ at $n=100$ to $48.4\times$ at $n=2\,000$.
The operational reason is structural, not incidental: every
update to the central node's cumulative closure sum invalidates the
candidate ratio of a large fraction of the remaining leaves at once, and
\texttt{HPaC}'s lazy heap must absorb that many stale entries before it can
pop a valid minimum again, while \texttt{PaC}'s direct scan pays no such
maintenance cost. This same mechanism is responsible for the far larger
effect at scale described in \cref{subsec:v2_structured}: on stars with
$n\ge 20\,000$, \texttt{HPaC} does not merely lose to \texttt{PaC}, it
exhausts the 8\,GB memory ceiling entirely.

\subsection{HPaC scaling on arbitrarily oriented forests}\label{subsec:v2_hfma_scaling}

On the large random campaign (C, $n\in\{10\,000,\dots,100\,000\}$,
mixed-forest, 2\,400 instances), \texttt{HPaC} stays fast in absolute terms:
median elapsed time grows from a few hundred microseconds at $n=10\,000$ to
under a second at $n=100\,000$ in the same runs used for
\cref{tab:v2_c_rac}, with no instance approaching the 300\,s timeout.
\texttt{DHPaC}, the dual general heap-based algorithm, is consistently
slower than \texttt{HPaC} on this campaign -- median
\texttt{DHPaC}/\texttt{HPaC} $\approx 1.6$--$1.7\times$, stable across every
size from $n=10\,000$ to $n=100\,000$ (\texttt{campaign\_c\_dhpac\_over\_hpac.tex}),
close to the $\approx 1.4$--$1.55\times$ already observed on the medium
campaign B. The two never swap places and neither changes which algorithm
family (heap-based general vs.\ \texttt{RaC}) is preferable; the gap is
attributed to scan-direction bookkeeping, not to a qualitatively different
algorithm.

\subsection{Specialized orientations}\label{subsec:v2_specialized}

Campaign E ($n\in\{100,\dots,1000\}\cup\{10\,000,\dots,100\,000\}$,
$\rho\in\{0.6,1.0\}$, 5 seeds, 1\,200 instances per orientation) compares the
specialized heap variants against the general algorithm on the orientation
each is built for. \texttt{HIPaC} is faster than \texttt{HPaC} on in-forests
at every size, with a median ratio \texttt{HIPaC}/\texttt{HPaC} between
$0.27$ and $0.50$ (i.e.\ \texttt{HIPaC} takes roughly a third to a half of
\texttt{HPaC}'s time), and \texttt{HOPaC} is faster than \texttt{HPaC} on
out-forests with a median ratio between $0.35$ and $0.59$
(\cref{tab:v2_e_specialized}). Both specializations retain their advantage
across the entire size range tested, including $n=100\,000$, with no sign of
the gain eroding at scale.

\subsection{General parametric-flow baseline}\label{subsec:v2_bppf}

Campaign F pairs \texttt{HPaC} and \texttt{RaC} against \texttt{BPPF}, an
unmodified upstream bounded-precision pseudoflow solver, on
$n\in\{100,200,500,1000\}$ mixed-forest instances across all six coefficient
families (72 instances). Because the \texttt{.pcf}-to-\texttt{BPPF} converter
bakes one exact rational $\lambda$ into integer arc capacities per call
(\cref{subsec:v2_validation}), \texttt{BPPF} is driven once per breakpoint
\texttt{HPaC} reports rather than swept natively; agreement is exact on
every one of the 72 instances (\textbf{zero mismatches} between
\texttt{BPPF}'s minimum-cut closure and \texttt{HPaC}'s at every sampled
breakpoint midpoint). The resulting total time -- summed over
one-process-per-breakpoint invocations -- is between $91\times$ and
$1\,270\times$ \texttt{HPaC}'s in-process time on the same instance; this
number is process-spawn-dominated by construction and is reported only as
evidence that \texttt{BPPF} is a correct, independent oracle at this scale,
never as a native-speed comparison with the in-process forest algorithms.

\subsection{HPaC versus RaC on random forests}\label{subsec:v2_hfma_vs_rac}

On the medium campaign (B), the median paired ratio \texttt{RaC}/\texttt{HPaC}
is stable between $2.6\times$ and $3.0\times$ across every size from
$n=100$ to $n=1\,000$ (\cref{tab:v2_b_rac}), with no strong dependence on
density or coefficient family. On the large campaign (C), the same ratio
grows steadily with $n$: from $3.1\times$ at $n=10\,000$ to $12.9\times$ at
$n=100\,000$ (\cref{tab:v2_c_rac}), with an increasingly wide interquartile
range at the largest sizes, meaning \texttt{RaC}'s relative disadvantage
becomes both larger and more variable as forests grow. \texttt{DHPaC}/\texttt{RaC}
shows the same qualitative trend as \texttt{HPaC}/\texttt{RaC} at every size
tested; the primal/dual choice never changes which algorithm family is
preferable on random forests. Every instance in both campaigns completed
well inside the timeout, with zero timeouts and zero memory censoring.

Mixed-orientation stars invert this picture completely, and do so
increasingly sharply as $n$ grows. On the sizes where both algorithms
survive ($n\le 10\,000$, \cref{tab:v2_star_rac}), median \texttt{RaC}/\texttt{HPaC}
falls from $0.424$ at $n=100$ to $0.002$ at $n=10\,000$: \texttt{RaC} is
already $2.4\times$ faster than \texttt{HPaC} in the median at $n=100$, and
roughly $500\times$ faster at $n=10\,000$. Beyond that size the comparison
becomes one-sided in a stronger sense: \emph{every one} of the 60 star
instances at $n=20\,000$, every one at $n=50\,000$, and every one at
$n=100\,000$ made both \texttt{HPaC} and \texttt{DHPaC} exceed the 8\,GB
memory ceiling (180 censored instances in total, recorded as
\texttt{status=oom} in the raw data, never estimated or imputed), while
\texttt{RaC} completed all 180 of the same instances comfortably, with a
median time of $0.593\,\mathrm{s}$ at $n=100\,000$ and no failures. A
single-repetition, single-algorithm reference measurement (Release build,
same machine) makes the memory picture concrete: at $n=100\,000$,
\texttt{HPaC} throws \texttt{std::bad\_alloc} after allocating on the order
of $25\,\mathrm{GB}$, whereas \texttt{RaC} peaks at $213\,\mathrm{MiB}$ on
the same instance. \texttt{RaC}'s own scaling on stars is close to linear
across three orders of magnitude, from $0.541\,\mathrm{ms}$ at $n=100$ to
$592\,\mathrm{ms}$ at $n=100\,000$ (\cref{tab:v2_star_rac_scaling}).

The operational explanation mirrors \cref{subsec:v2_fma_vs_hfma}: every
update to the star's central node invalidates a large fraction of the
remaining leaves' candidate ratios at once, so \texttt{HPaC}'s lazy heap
accumulates stale entries at a rate that grows with $n$ itself, not just
with the number of genuine breakpoints; \texttt{RaC}'s rake operations
absorb the star's leaves in a small number of rounds regardless of the
central node's update history, which is exactly the structural case
rake-and-compress is built for.

\subsection{Computational conclusions}\label{subsec:v2_conclusions}

Correctness is uniform across the entire study: 9\,000 benchmarked instances,
203\,880 timed runs, seven algorithms, zero disagreements, plus 481
independent breakpoint-midpoint cross-checks against \texttt{BPPF}
(\cref{subsec:v2_validation}) and exact agreement on every one of the 72
campaign-F instances (\cref{subsec:v2_bppf}). No algorithm in this study
returned an incorrect sequence on any instance we generated.

\texttt{HPaC} (and its dual \texttt{DHPaC}, consistently $1.4$--$1.7\times$
slower but never changing the qualitative picture) is the preferred general
algorithm on mixed-orientation random forests, paths and balanced binary
trees, with its advantage over \texttt{RaC} growing from a stable
$2$--$3\times$ at medium size to over $12\times$ at $n=100\,000$ on random
forests. \texttt{RaC} is the preferred algorithm on mixed-orientation stars,
where its advantage over \texttt{HPaC} also grows with $n$ -- from
$\approx 2\times$ at $n=100$ to $\approx 500\times$ at $n=10\,000$ -- and
becomes categorical beyond $n=20\,000$, where \texttt{HPaC} is not merely
slower but exhausts an 8\,GB memory ceiling on every instance tested while
\texttt{RaC} continues to scale near-linearly with a memory footprint two
orders of magnitude smaller. \texttt{HIPaC} and \texttt{HOPaC} deliver a
stable $2$--$3\times$ speedup over the general \texttt{HPaC} on in-forests
and out-forests respectively, at every size tested up to $n=100\,000$, with
no sign of the gain closing at scale. \texttt{BPPF}, used here purely as an
independent verification oracle and a correctness-scoped baseline, agrees
with every algorithm in this study on every instance tested; the domains are
different by construction (general graphs vs.\ forests), so no relative
performance claim between \texttt{BPPF} and the forest-specific algorithms is
made beyond the explicitly process-spawn-dominated numbers in
\cref{subsec:v2_bppf}. No algorithm in this study is described as "state of
the art": each claim above is scoped to the topology, size range and
coefficient families in which it was actually measured.
